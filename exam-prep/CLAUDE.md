9. **L-Systems.** Define one first, in the lecture's own notation: an L-System is the **4-tuple $(V, C, w, P)$** — $V$ the variables (symbols that may be replaced), $C$ the constants (symbols that stay), $A = V \cup C$ the alphabet, $w \in A$ the axiom, $P$ the production rules $A \to A^m$ — and the basic form is the **D0L-System**, *deterministic* and *zero-context* (context-free), with all symbols rewritten **in parallel** at every step. Then the construction task: given a target string at a given $t$, invent alphabet + axiom + ≤4 rules and **verify by expanding $t=0\to1\to2\to3$**. The recurring target is `OAOAOA…O` from axiom `O` at $t=3$ — but **check the length**: the 2017 and 2023 papers ask for the 15-symbol `OAOAOAOAOAOAOAO` while `qn-01` records the 13-symbol `OAOAOAOAOAOAO`. A rule pair like $O\to OAO,\;A\to A$ gives $1\to3\to7\to15$; solve whichever length the paper actually prints, and say which one you assumed. Sheet-04 has the harder 32-symbol / 3-rule variant (`ABBCBCCABCCACAABBCCACAABCAABABBC` at $t{=}5$ from axiom `A`). Two extras from the deck that make good second sentences: the lecture's own Child/Adult example ($V=\{C,A\}$, axiom `C`, $C\to A$, $A\to CA$) produces string lengths $1,1,2,3,5,8,13,21$ — **the Fibonacci sequence**, which links this topic straight to `lect-05`; and the **turtle-graphics** convention (`+` turn left by $\alpha$, `-` turn right, `[` push the current position, `]` pop it) turns a string into a plant, which is what the L-System was invented for. Know that extensions exist — bracketed, context-dependent, stochastic, parametric. *(2017 Q11, qn-02 16, qn-03, qn-01 T16; oral A, H; sheet-04)*
10. **Fibonacci — two variants.** (a) **↔ golden ratio:** the exam wants a *derivation*, and there are two, so have one and recognise the other. **The sheet's route** ($\texttt{sheet-05}$): set $\beta=\lim F_{n+1}/F_n$, divide $F_{n+2}=F_{n+1}+F_n$ by $F_{n+1}$ to get $\beta = 1 + 1/\beta$, so $\beta^2-\beta-1=0$ and $\beta=\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\approx1.618$. **The lecture's route**, which is geometric: the golden ratio divides an interval so that *the whole is to the longer part as the longer is to the shorter*, i.e. $1/x = x/(1-x) \Rightarrow x^2+x-1=0$, whose roots are $\varphi\approx1.618033988$ and $\rho = 1/\varphi \approx 0.618033988$. Quote the definition $x_{i+1}=x_i+x_{i-1}$ with $x_0=0, x_1=1$, its origin (Leonardo of Pisa, 1202, the rabbit-pair population), and add the numeric table (1, 2, 1.5, 1.667, 1.6, 1.625, …) as the "exemplary calculation". Memorise the identities the slide lists — $\varphi - \rho = 1$, $\varphi\rho = 1$, $1/\varphi = \varphi-1$, $1/\rho = \rho+1$, $\varphi^2 = 1+\varphi$, $\rho^2 = 1-\rho$ — each is a free extra line. (b) **vs logistic growth (2017 Q16):** give both formulas — Fibonacci growing geometrically with ratio $\varphi$ and therefore **unbounded** (the lecture's framing: *unbounded reproduction is not realistic*), versus **logistic growth**, where the growth rate is throttled by the resources the population has left over, $g = a(M - x_i)$, giving $x_{i+1} = x_i + a(M-x_i)x_i$ — Verhulst, 1838 — which rises almost exponentially, inflects at $x = \tfrac12 M$, and converges to the resource ceiling $M$; the continuous version $dP/dt = P(1-P)$ solves to the sigmoid $P(t) = 1/(1+e^{-t})$, known in neural networks as the sigmoid or **Fermi function**. The **logistic map** $x_{i+1}=a\,x_i(1-x_i)$ is the lecture's "special version" of it. Compare on those terms: unbounded geometric vs self-limiting sigmoid. *(2017 Q16, qn-02 15, qn-03, qn-01 T18; sheet-05)*
11. **Behaviour classes, attractors and the logistic map's exact thresholds.** From dynamical-systems theory the lecture names four **classes of behaviour** — divergence, convergence, periodic behaviour, deterministic chaos — and the matching **attractors**: infinity, fixpoint (stable or unstable), limit cycle (stable or unstable), and the **strange attractor**, often of fractal dimension. Be able to attach each class to a logistic-map parameter range, with the numbers, because the examiner wants exact values: $0<a<1$ decays to 0; $1<a<3$ converges to a fixpoint $x^*$; $3<a<3.44949$ oscillates between 2 values; beyond $3.44949$ the period doubles to 4, 8, 16 …; $3.56995<a<4$ is the onset of deterministic chaos; $a=4$ is fully chaotic; $a>4$ diverges. Derive the fixpoints by setting $x^*_{i+1}=x^*_i$ — giving $x^*=0$ and $x^*=1-\tfrac1a$ — and be ready to **draw $x^*$ against $a$** over $0<a<4$, which is exactly what `sheet-05` demands. Name the accompanying phenomena the slide lists: the period-doubling route to chaos, self-similarity, positive Lyapunov exponents, fractal dimension, Hopf and pitchfork bifurcations. Two neighbours worth one sentence each: **sensitivity to initial conditions** (`sheet-05` runs $x_0 = 0.228734167$ against $0.228734168$ at $a = 3.3, 3.51, 3.75$ — the point is that the trajectories diverge although the rule is deterministic, i.e. **Wolfram class III**), and the **Look-and-Say sequence** (Conway; identical in idea to run-length encoding; the starting digit stays at the end; it grows indefinitely except for `22`; from digits 0–3 nothing above 3 ever appears; and the length ratio tends to **Conway's constant $\lambda \approx 1.303577$**, about 30 % growth per step). *(sheet-05; supports 2017 Q16 and the class III/IV pool question)*
12. **Pattern formation — the `lect-05` wildcard block.** Lectured in full and examined orally in 2010, but **absent from all four written papers**, so learn the minimum viable answer and no more. Four items, one equation and one sketch each. **Predator-prey / Lotka-Volterra:** iterated form $N_{i+1}=N_i+aN_i-bN_iP_i$ and $P_{i+1}=P_i+cP_iN_i-dP_i$, or as coupled differential equations $\partial N/\partial t = N(a-bP)$ and $\partial P/\partial t = P(cN-d)$; name every symbol ($N$ prey, $P$ predators, $a$ prey birth rate, $b$ hunting loss, $c$ predator gain from food, $d$ predator death rate), state the result — a **periodic oscillation** with the predator curve lagging the prey curve — and cite the Hudson-Bay lynx/hare records (1845–1935) as the empirical support. **Activator-inhibitor:** $X_{i+1}=X_i+aX_i-bY_i$ (activator) and $Y_{i+1}=Y_i+cX_i-dY_i$ (inhibitor); the activator drives its own growth *and* the inhibitor's, while the inhibitor damps its own growth *and* the activator's; predator-prey is one member of this family. Examples: morphogenesis, tiger stripes and sea shells (Turing, 1952), heartbeat and locomotion rhythms, antagonistic flexor-extensor muscle pairs. **Reaction-diffusion:** add a spatial term, $dV/dt = \mu\,\partial^2V/\partial x^2 + f(V,W)$ and $dW/dt = \sigma\,\partial^2W/\partial x^2 + f_W$ — diffusion couples a compartment to its neighbours, reaction is the local dynamics. The examinable punchline is the link back to week 2: **a cellular automaton is a discretized reaction-diffusion system**, because the rule *is* the reaction term and the neighbourhood *is* the diffusion term. Belousov-Zhabotinsky is the standard chemical demonstration. **Phyllotaxis and the golden angle:** each new leaf grows where the inhibitor left by existing leaves is weakest, which pushes successive leaves toward a separation of **137.51°**, the golden angle; the visible spiral counts in pinecones and sunflowers are **consecutive Fibonacci numbers**. Close the loop with **self-similarity** — the same property shows up in plant morphogenesis, L-Systems, CA, deterministic chaos and iterated functions. *(orals A, E; no written paper — treat as insurance, not as a priority)*
13. **SOC scaling law — formula and plot.** Write $N(s)=a\,s^{-\alpha}$, define every symbol ($s$ = size/strength of an event, $N(s)$ = number of events of that size, $\alpha$ = characteristic exponent), then show $\log N = \log a - \alpha \log s$ ⇒ a straight, falling line in a log–log plot. Prove **scale invariance** ($f(\lambda s)=C(\lambda)f(s)$, sheet-06). Then instantiate it: **Gutenberg-Richter** $\log_{10}N = a - bM$ with $b\approx1$, axes drawn and labelled; or sandpile avalanches, Zipf, Lotka. *(2017 Q13, qn-01 T5; oral C, E, H; sheet-06)*
14. **Pheromone decay.** Exponential decay $\tau(t)=\tau_0\rho^{\,t}$; solve $\rho^{42}=0.1 \Rightarrow \rho=0.1^{1/42}\approx0.9462$. Then explain why evaporation is essential in a *dynamic* environment. *(sheet-06)*
15. **EA mutation probability.** Per-bit flip probability $p$; an offspring is unchanged with probability $(1-p)^L$. **At least one of $N$ offspring differs from the parent:** $Q = 1-(1-p)^{NL}$. **No offspring is identical to the parent:** $Q=\bigl(1-(1-p)^L\bigr)^{N}$. Substitute $p=1/L^2$ when asked. **Read the question twice** — the papers contain both phrasings and they are different formulas. Sanity-check the edge cases the orals probe: $p=0$ ⇒ no change, $p=1$ ⇒ every bit flips (deterministic complement, no diversity), $p=0.5$ ⇒ pure random restart, small $p$ ⇒ useful local search. *(qn-01 T13, qn-02 4, qn-03; orals A, F)*
16. **Wheel of Fortune probability.** Rank-based shares with ranks $1..P$: $\omega_i = \dfrac{P-r(i)+1}{\sum_{j=1}^{P} j} = \dfrac{2\,(P-r(i)+1)}{P(P+1)}$, so the best individual gets $\omega_1 = \dfrac{2}{P+1}$. Then "best individual among $\rho$ draws" $=1-(1-\omega_1)^{\rho}$ for drawing with replacement. *(qn-01 T11, qn-02 9, qn-03; sheet-09)*
17. **Selection & performance diagrams — two different plots, do not confuse them.**
18. **Full EA task (10 pt) — two shapes.** (a) **Applied design** (2023, 2025): name and explain **every** EA part and step, give pros and cons, then instantiate all of it for the soda-recipe problem — genome layout under the constraints (6–7 base ingredients as reals; up to 15 of 60–70 additives; each additive ≤1 %, all additives together ≤3 %), a **pairwise-comparison** fitness from the student tasting panel (⇒ tournament / rank-based selection, because the panel yields relative not absolute fitness), repair or penalty for constraint violation, chosen selection/recombination/mutation operators, and a stopping criterion. 19. **EA follow-ups worth one extra sentence each** (asked repeatedly in the orals, natural sub-parts of a written 5-pointer): **termination criteria** (fixed iterations, fixed time/budget, target quality reached, stagnation of best fitness, population converged/super-individual); **the most expensive step** (fitness evaluation, and if that answer is ruled out: external selection $O(n\log n)$ sorting, parent selection up to $O(n^2)$, mutation $O(n)$ random numbers); **$(\mu+\lambda)$ vs $(\mu,\lambda)$** ($+$ keeps parents ⇒ best fitness is monotonically non-decreasing and saves re-evaluations, but risks stagnation; $(\mu,\lambda)$ discards parents ⇒ escapes local optima, and is the right choice when fitness **drifts over time**); **super-individual** (one individual so much fitter that it takes over the whole population ⇒ diversity collapse; avoided by rank-based instead of fitness-proportional selection, softmax scaling, or capping offspring per parent); **why EAs are popular** (no gradient or model of the objective needed, anytime behaviour — a usable answer at any interruption, trivially parallel, applicable to discrete and mixed genomes). *(orals B, C, D, F, H; sheet-08, sheet-09)*
20. **Braitenberg vehicles.** Type 1 (one sensor, one motor; the stochastic component and why noise helps); types 2a/2b/3a/3b wiring and the resulting fear / aggression / love / explorer behaviours; **type 3b obstacle avoidance** drawn as a scene with vehicle *and* obstacle (2017 Q9 demands the scene); **what changes when proximity sensors are replaced by distance sensors** — the sensor characteristic inverts (near⇒large becomes near⇒small), so the inhibitory mapping inverts and avoidance turns into approach/collision; 3b corner traps and at least two fixes (stochastic component, steeper sensor-motor function, memory). 21. **Didabots (10 pt).** The setup (a simple two-front-sensor robot in an arena of movable boxes), its actual task (**obstacle avoidance only — there is no clustering rule**), the observed result (**boxes end up in heaps/clusters**), and the reason: a box seen head-on falls in the sensors' blind spot, so the robot pushes it until a side sensor triggers and it turns away, depositing the box beside existing boxes. Then **one vs many Didabots**: with several robots clustering is faster and clusters grow larger and fewer, though robots also interfere with and re-scatter each other's heaps. 22. **Boids (Reynolds).** The three local rules — **separation** (steer away from crowding neighbours), **alignment** (match neighbours' average heading), **cohesion** (steer toward neighbours' average position) — each explained in one sentence, with a sketch, plus the point that all three use only a **local neighbourhood** and that swarming is emergent. Be ready for the extensions: obstacle avoidance, and adding a **goal/target term** so the flock pursues a position or an objective optimum (sheet-11). *(2017 Q12; oral F; sheet-11)*
23. **PSO.** Velocity update $v_i \leftarrow w\,v_i + c_1 R_1 (p_i^{\text{best}} - x_i) + c_2 R_2 (g^{\text{best}} - x_i)$ and position update $x_i \leftarrow x_i + v_i$ — 2017 Q10 asked for the **position** update, so give both and define every symbol ($w$ inertia, $c_1,c_2$ acceleration constants, $R_1,R_2$ independent uniform random numbers in $[0,1]$, $p_i^{\text{best}}$ the particle's own best position, $g^{\text{best}}$ the best in the swarm/neighbourhood). Say what $R$ is for (stochastic weighting so particles do not follow identical trajectories) and **which term is which**: the $p^{\text{best}}$ term plus $R$ is exploration, the $g^{\text{best}}$ term is exploitation, inertia carries momentum. Know the **ring topology** variant where $g^{\text{best}}$ is replaced by the best of the $r=1$ neighbourhood, and do not confuse PSO with Boids. *(2017 Q10; oral H; sheet-12)*
24. **Subsumption (Brooks).** The layered architecture in which each layer is a complete behaviour and higher layers subsume lower ones; the **two top-down mechanisms — suppression, which replaces a signal on an *input* line, and inhibition, which blocks a signal on an *output* line** — each drawn as a labelled circle on the line, with the number inside the circle explained as the time constant for which the effect lasts. *(qn-01 T7; sheet-12)*
25. **Exploration vs exploitation — the cross-cutting question.** Be able to locate both, in one sentence each, in **EAs** (mutation & random init vs selection, elitism & recombination), the **ant algorithm** (probabilistic path choice & pheromone evaporation vs pheromone reinforcement of good paths), and **PSO** (the random $R$ terms & inertia vs the pull toward $g^{\text{best}}$). It has been asked directly about all three. *(2017 Q3; orals F, H; sheets 06, 08, 12)*

## TEACHING STYLE (the one standard for writing `notes/phase-N.md`)

Pen-and-paper exam: only what I can write and draw scores. Teach me as a total beginner, but keep it fast to read — I have ~16 hours for all four phases. No exceptions:

1. **Teach from zero, every step shown.** Build each concept in order: the problem it solves → the intuition (one analogy if it helps) → the mechanism → a worked example → the exam answer. Expand every acronym and define every term on first use, and recap a prerequisite before the thing that needs it (neighbourhood before rule table; rule table before Wolfram number; genome before mutation). **Never skip a derivation step or call one "obvious": whenever a formula, exponent, fraction, or number appears, show where each part comes from** (e.g. exactly why the rule table has $k^{2r+1}$ lines and not $k^{2r+1}$ rules).
2. **Format for speed, not a novel.** Lead a topic with **2–3 plain sentences**, then use **bullet points** for any list of steps / components / phases / properties, and **tables** for comparisons. Do not write paragraph after paragraph. Every bullet must be a **grammatically complete sentence with a subject and a verb** — write "A totalistic **rule** maps the neighbourhood onto the next state using only the sum of its states." NOT the fragment "totalistic: only the sum". Sub-points are also full sentences. Keep real multi-paragraph prose only for genuinely connected reasoning.
3. **Maths is pure LaTeX.** Inline `$…$`, multi-line `$$\begin{aligned}…\end{aligned}$$`; real tokens only (`\sum`, `\prod`, `\lim`, `\binom`, `\le`, `\approx`, `\sqrt{5}`, `x_{i+1}`, `k^{2r+1}`), never ASCII/unicode maths (`k^(2r+1)`, `x*`, `>=`, `sqrt5`). Show every substitution line, right-hand side included.
4. **Drawings are answers, not decoration.** This exam is drawing-heavy. Every CA evolution, glider, Langton's Ant step, Braitenberg wiring, subsumption block, fitness distribution and log–log plot must appear as an **ASCII-art grid, an ASCII diagram, or a mermaid diagram in a code fence**, labelled exactly as I should label it on paper. For grids, use a fixed convention (`.` = dead/white, `#` = alive/black) and state it once. Where the source slide clearly had a picture that the text dump lost, reconstruct it and say so.
5. **Anchor to the exam: verbatim question, then answer, in order.** Paste each past-exam task's wording from the four written papers — `combined_papers.md` Part 2 (2017), `qn-02`, `qn-03`, `qn-01` — noting that each is a memory protocol, then its worked answer immediately below, part (a)→(a), (b)→(b); never replace an answer with "(see §X)". Solve the actual question with its own data. **Where a pool question appears on several papers with different data, solve every variant, labelled** (`✅ 2017 Q8` vs `✅ 2025 T9`), because the variation is exactly what will change again.
6. **Flag relevance honestly.** Head each topic with its written-paper recurrence: `> POOL 4/4 — 2017 Q5, qn-02 6, qn-03, qn-01 T1`, or `> POOL 1/4 — 2017 Q10 only`, or `> NOT on any written paper — lectured, wildcard`, or `> CUT — not in the SS26 syllabus, do not study`. Four written papers justify "recurring" and "expected", never "guaranteed". Mark which sub-parts earn independent points. Never say "skip" for live material — always give the minimum viable answer to write.
7. **Deliver + close.** Write the full teaching into `notes/phase-N.md`; in chat reply only a short summary. End each topic with an **Exam-Ready Checklist** and a 3–5 question **active-recall quiz with answers**, at least one of which requires a drawing.
8. **Teach like I know absolutely nothing.** Assume the reader is a beginner in complex systems, biology, and optimization. Never assume I know what a lattice, alphabet, transition rule, genome, allele, fitness landscape, fixpoint, attractor, log–log plot, stigmergy, or emergence means. Every new term gets one simple defining sentence before it is used, and every formula is unpacked symbol by symbol. Whenever you write an equation — inline like $Z = k^{L}$ or displayed — **immediately explain what every symbol represents** ("$k$ is the number of states a single cell may take, $L$ is the number of lines in the rule table, and the exponentiation is there because each line is filled independently"). Every number, exponent, index, or substitution must be justified by showing where it came from. Never write "substitute", "plug in", "it follows", or "obviously" without first explaining why.
9. **NON-NEGOTIABLE:** when a topic is taught, **every exercise-sheet assignment for that topic is solved**, and **every past-exam task for that topic is solved across all four written papers**. Where my sheet's stored solution disagrees with your derivation, say so explicitly and mark the correction — **the sheets are student work and are not a trusted source; only their questions are.**

**Fallbacks for unseen or varied questions:** "explain mechanism X" = purpose → components → rules/steps → an example → what emerges from it; a calculation you cannot finish = write the formula, substitute, show the method (partial credit); an unsure comparison = a 2-column table (each row scores); a "draw it" you are unsure about = draw the labelled axes/grid anyway and annotate. Never leave a sub-part blank — naming the mechanism earns points.
