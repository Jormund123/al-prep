\section*{Assignment 73 (3 Points)}
\begin{problem}
What are the major differences between the following groups of animals: flocks, herds and
swarms? What difference with respect to the behavior of the individuals can you determine
in these groups?
\end{problem}
\begin{answer}
The three terms denote group types differing in species, medium and internal structure:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Flock:} group of \emph{birds} (e.g.\ geese, auklets, cormorants) moving in air.
        Often spatially structured (e.g.\ V-formation) and strongly motion-coordinated:
        individuals continuously align heading and speed with nearby neighbours.
  \item \textbf{Herd:} group of (mostly large) \emph{land mammals} (e.g.\ zebra, gnus, penguins
        on the ground). Movement on a 2D surface; individuals stay together mainly for
        protection/foraging, coordination is looser and often hierarchically influenced
        (lead animals).
  \item \textbf{Swarm:} a large, often very dense group of (typically small / simple)
        animals or insects (e.g.\ locusts, fish schools, jellyfish). Usually homogeneous,
        no hierarchy and no fixed individual roles; global behaviour emerges purely from
        many local interactions.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Differences in individual behavior:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Flock/School:} each individual actively coordinates velocity and direction with
        local neighbours $\rightarrow$ tight, polarised, aligned motion.
  \item \emph{Herd:} individuals are more autonomous; cohesion is driven by safety/resources,
        alignment is weaker, position within the group can matter (centre = safer).
  \item \emph{Swarm:} individuals follow very simple local rules with no central control and no
        knowledge of the global pattern; ``intelligent'' collective behaviour is purely
        \emph{emergent}.
\end{itemize}
In short: flocks/schools are highly velocity-aligned, herds are loosely cohesive and may be
structured/hierarchical, swarms are homogeneous and self-organised through local interaction only.
\end{answer}

\section*{Assignment 74 (2 Points)}
\begin{problem}
In the Didabot experiment the Didabots are creating so called heaps or clusters of boxes.
Give a definition of a cluster and try to formulate a mathematical criterion that can decide
if an arrangement of boxes has clusters or not.
\end{problem}
\begin{answer}
\textbf{Definition.} A \emph{cluster} (heap) is a group of two or more boxes lying so close
together that each box of the group is in direct contact with, or within a small neighbourhood
distance of, at least one other box of the same group. A single isolated box is not a cluster.

\textbf{Mathematical criterion.} Let the boxes be $B_1,\dots,B_n$ with centre positions
$\mathbf{p}_i \in \mathbb{R}^2$. Fix a threshold distance $\varepsilon$ (e.g.\ a small multiple
of the box size). Define two boxes as \emph{neighbours} if
\[
  \lVert \mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j \rVert \;\le\; \varepsilon , \qquad i \neq j .
\]
Build the graph $G=(V,E)$ with $V=\{B_1,\dots,B_n\}$ and
\[
  E=\bigl\{\, (i,j) \;:\; \lVert \mathbf{p}_i-\mathbf{p}_j\rVert \le \varepsilon \,\bigr\}.
\]
A \textbf{cluster} is then a \emph{connected component} of $G$ containing at least two vertices.

\textbf{Decision.} An arrangement \emph{contains clusters} iff $G$ has at least one connected
component of size $\ge 2$, i.e.\ iff
\[
  \exists\, i \neq j : \lVert \mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j \rVert \le \varepsilon .
\]
Equivalently: if $\deg(B_i)=0$ for all $i$ (no edges), there are no clusters; otherwise clusters
exist. The number of clusters equals the number of connected components of size $\ge 2$.
\end{answer}

\section*{Assignment 75 (3 Points)}
\begin{problem}
How will the resulting clusters be affected in the Didabot experiment when changing from one
Didabot to multiple Didabots? Explain your solution.
\end{problem}
\begin{answer}
With a \emph{single} Didabot, two release mechanisms produce the heaps:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Spontaneous release:} the curved nose plus natural jiggling moves a carried box
        sideways into a sensor's range; the 3b behavior turns the robot and drops the box at an
        arbitrary position.
  \item \textbf{Induced release:} the robot meets an obstacle or another box, the 3b avoidance
        turns it and the carried box is dropped \emph{next to} that obstacle/box $\rightarrow$
        heaps grow.
\end{itemize}
With \textbf{multiple identical Didabots}, each robot still performs type-3b obstacle avoidance,
box pushing and heap building, and both mechanisms above remain active. In addition a
\textbf{third mechanism} appears:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Induced release type 2:} when two Didabots approach each other, each detects the
        other as an obstacle and performs the 3b avoiding turn, thereby releasing the boxes they
        are currently carrying.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Effect on the clusters:} Cluster formation becomes \emph{faster} (more robots
push/redistribute boxes in parallel and the extra release events add up). The process is more
robust and the qualitative result (heaps in the interior and along the boundary) is unchanged,
but the additional robot--robot induced releases create more drop events, so the final number,
size and distribution of heaps differ from the single-robot case.
\end{answer}

\section*{Assignment 76 (2 Points)}
\begin{problem}
Find and cite a recent application of swarming behavior in the field of movie industry.
Give a scientifically acceptable citation.
\end{problem}
\begin{answer}
A classic and well documented application of flocking/swarming behavior in film is the
\emph{Massive} crowd-simulation system, which used autonomous agents (in the spirit of
Reynolds' boids) to animate the large battle crowds in Peter Jackson's
\emph{The Lord of the Rings} trilogy. The boids steering model is the foundation of such
crowd/flock animation in the movie industry.

\textbf{Citation:}\\
Reynolds, C.\,W. (1987). \emph{Flocks, Herds, and Schools: A Distributed Behavioral Model}.
In \emph{Computer Graphics} (SIGGRAPH '87 Conference Proceedings), 21(4), pp.~25--34.

\noindent A more application-oriented reference:\\
Reynolds, C.\,W. (1999). \emph{Steering Behaviors for Autonomous Characters}. In
\emph{Proceedings of the Game Developers Conference} (GDC), pp.~763--782.
\end{answer}

\section*{Assignment 77 (2 Points)}
\begin{problem}
Explain a set of rules for autonomous agents that is capable of generating swarm-like behavior.
Please use your own words for the explanation.
\end{problem}
\begin{answer}
Craig Reynolds' boids model generates flocking from three simple local steering rules. Each
agent only looks at its \emph{nearby} flockmates (those within a limited perception radius) and
adjusts its own velocity:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Separation:} steer away from neighbours that are too close, so the agents do not
        collide or crowd together.
  \item \textbf{Alignment:} steer so that the agent's heading and speed match the average
        velocity (average heading) of its local neighbours.
  \item \textbf{Cohesion:} steer towards the average position (centre of mass) of the local
        neighbours, keeping the group together.
\end{itemize}
At each time step every agent computes the three steering vectors, combines them (typically a
weighted sum) and updates its velocity and position. There is no leader and no central control:
each agent acts only on local information, yet the combination of avoiding, aligning and grouping
makes a coherent, fluid swarm motion \emph{emerge} globally.
\end{answer}

\section*{Assignment 78 (3 Points)}
\begin{problem}
The original set of rules from Craig Reynolds made the Boids move aimlessly. Now consider that
we would like them to move towards a goal. Propose an extension to the original Boids idea that
can make the Boids pursue a goal (a special position in space, or a maximum/minimum of a given
objective function, etc.). Feel free to propose ideas of your own.
\end{problem}
\begin{answer}
The three original rules (separation, alignment, cohesion) only produce internally coherent but
directionless motion. To make the swarm pursue a goal we add a \textbf{fourth steering rule}
that contributes its own velocity term to the weighted sum.

\textbf{(a) Goal-seeking towards a fixed target $\mathbf{g}$.}
For an agent at position $\mathbf{p}_i$ define a desired velocity pointing at the goal,
\[
  \mathbf{v}^{\text{goal}}_i \;=\; v_{\max}\,
  \frac{\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{p}_i}{\lVert \mathbf{g}-\mathbf{p}_i \rVert},
\]
and add it to the existing rules:
\[
  \mathbf{v}_i \;\leftarrow\; w_s\,\mathbf{v}^{\text{sep}}_i
  + w_a\,\mathbf{v}^{\text{ali}}_i
  + w_c\,\mathbf{v}^{\text{coh}}_i
  + w_g\,\mathbf{v}^{\text{goal}}_i ,
\]
with weights $w_s,w_a,w_c,w_g$. The goal weight $w_g$ controls how strongly the swarm is pulled
towards $\mathbf{g}$ while the other three rules keep it a cohesive, collision-free group.

\textbf{(b) Optimising an objective function $f(\mathbf{p})$.}
If instead we want the boids to find a maximum/minimum of an objective function, replace the
goal direction by the local \emph{gradient}:
\[
  \mathbf{v}^{\text{goal}}_i \;=\; \pm\, \nabla f(\mathbf{p}_i)
  \quad (\,+\text{ for a maximum},\ -\text{ for a minimum}\,).
\]
Each agent thus climbs/descends the function while separation, alignment and cohesion keep the
swarm together. If the gradient is unknown, each boid can be steered towards the best position
found among itself and its neighbours (a particle-swarm-optimisation style rule using a local
best and a global best), which lets the swarm collectively search for the optimum.

In both cases the key idea is the same: \emph{add an extra goal-/gradient-steering term to the
combined velocity}, so the swarm keeps its emergent flocking structure but now moves with purpose.
\end{answer}
